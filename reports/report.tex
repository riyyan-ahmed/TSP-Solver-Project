\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report presents our C++ solver for the metric Travelling Salesman Problem, written for the M1 AI programming project. The program reads both TSPLIB formats of the provided instances, computes a tour with the method chosen on the command line and writes it in the required tour format. Besides the trivial method, it offers the exact Held-Karp algorithm, which stores sets of cities as bitmasks, and two fast heuristics: a tour is first built from a minimum spanning tree or by always moving to the nearest city, and is then shortened with 2-opt. Held-Karp returns the published optimum on every provided instance of up to 24 cities, its limit. On the 15 provided instances, the two heuristics give tours that are on average 5.6\,\% and 5.7\,\% longer than the optimum, and they solve the largest instance, \texttt{pr1002}, in under 40\,ms. All 157 automated tests pass. We describe how we organised the work, how the code is structured and why, the algorithms and their complexity, the tests, and the performance evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Travelling Salesman Problem asks for the shortest round trip that visits every city of a list exactly once and returns to its start. The problem is NP-hard: exact methods take exponential time, so practical solvers rely on heuristics that find good, but not always optimal, tours. As in the subject~\cite{subject}, we consider the metric case, where the graph is complete and the distances satisfy the triangle inequality.

Our goal was a program that reads an instance in the TSPLIB format, solves it with a method chosen by the user and writes the tour to a file, together with a test suite and a build script that follow the specification exactly. Half of the grade rewards the quality of the design, so we split the program into small classes with one clear job each, connected through interfaces. The mid-term version (18 October) already read both input formats and ran the trivial method. This final version adds the Held-Karp algorithm, two heuristics based on 2-opt, the official TSPLIB distance functions and a reproducible performance evaluation.

Section~\ref{sec-compliance} shows that every requirement of the subject is met. Section~\ref{sec-team} describes how we worked, Section~\ref{sec-run} how to build and run the program, Sections~\ref{sec-design} to~\ref{sec-complexity} the design and the algorithms, and Sections~\ref{sec-testing} and~\ref{sec-perf} the tests and the measurements.

\subsection{Choosing the algorithms}\label{sec-choice}

The subject leaves the third method open and asks us to discuss it with the teacher before coding. On 19 October we studied the methods it suggests (Table~\ref{tab-candidates}). Understanding them well enough to choose turned out to be harder than we expected (challenge 6 in Table~\ref{tab-challenges}).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{3.1cm} Y Y@{}}
\toprule
Method & Idea & Our assessment \\
\midrule
2-opt local search & Start from any tour and replace two edges by two shorter ones, reversing the path between them, until no such swap helps & Simple and deterministic, easy to test. Needs a starting tour. \textbf{Chosen} \\
Nearest neighbour, MST tour & Starting tours: always move to the closest unvisited city, or visit a minimum spanning tree in depth-first order & Both in $O(n^2)$. The MST tour is at most twice the optimum on metric instances. \textbf{Chosen} as starting tours \\
Ant colony optimisation & Artificial ants build tours, guided by the pheromone left on short edges & Random, with several parameters to tune (number of ants, evaporation); results change from run to run \\
Simulated annealing & Random changes, sometimes accepting a longer tour with a probability that decreases over time & Random, needs a cooling schedule; no exact result to test \\
Genetic algorithm & A population of tours combined by crossover and mutation & Random, many parameters, and the crossover must keep every tour valid \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Methods we compared for the third algorithm.}
\label{tab-candidates}
\end{table}

\noindent We chose to build a tour from a minimum spanning tree or by nearest neighbour, and then improve it with 2-opt, for four reasons:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{We understood it completely.} Each step is a classic algorithm that we could explain and check by hand: Prim's algorithm for the tree, a depth-first walk for the order, the nearest neighbour rule and the 2-opt move.
  \item \textbf{It fits our design.} Building a tour and improving a tour are two separate jobs, so each one got its own interface (\texttt{TourConstructor}, \texttt{TourImprover}) and one solver combines them. Two constructions and one improvement give four methods (\texttt{mst}, \texttt{mst-2opt}, \texttt{nn}, \texttt{nn-2opt}) for little extra code.
  \item \textbf{It comes with a guarantee.} On metric instances the MST tour is at most twice the weight of the tree, and therefore at most twice the optimum; our tests check this bound on every coordinate instance. Nearest neighbour has no such guarantee, which makes the comparison between the two interesting.
  \item \textbf{It is deterministic and polynomial.} The same input always gives the same tour, so the tests can expect exact tours, and each construction and each 2-opt pass costs $O(n^2)$, which is fast enough for \texttt{pr1002}.
\end{itemize}
The metaheuristics, in contrast, are random, depend on parameters, and would have needed many runs to evaluate fairly; we could not have implemented and tested them as well in the time we had. We presented this choice to the teacher, who approved it before we started coding.

For Held-Karp, we studied existing implementations on 20 October. All of them fill a table indexed by a subset of cities and an end city; they differ in how they store the subsets. We chose bitmasks, which we already knew from the subset lab of the course: city 0 is the fixed start, and any set of the other cities is one integer whose bit $k$ says whether city $k+1$ belongs to it. Membership, insertion and removal are then single bit operations, and the integer itself is the row of the table. Section~\ref{sec-hk} describes the table and the order in which it is filled.

\subsection{Tools and libraries}

The solver uses only the C++20 standard library. \subsection{Parsing}\label{sec-parsing}

\texttt{TspParser} reads the header line by line, splits each line at its first \texttt{:} and trims both sides, until it meets the keyword of a data section. It then checks that \texttt{NAME}, \texttt{TYPE: TSP} and a positive \texttt{DIMENSION} are present. The keyword selects one of two readers, each in its own file. \texttt{LowerDiagParser.cpp} reads the weights as a stream of integers, because the rows of a TSPLIB matrix may wrap over several lines, fills both halves of the matrix, and skips the \texttt{DISPLAY\_DATA\_SECTION} found in \texttt{dantzig42}. \texttt{CoordinateParser.cpp} reads all the lines first and then stores city $k$ at index $k$ minus the smallest identifier, which is 1 in TSPLIB files and 0 in files from \texttt{instance\_generator.py}; this also handles lines in any order. The parser is strict about anything that changes the instance (negative weights, a non-zero diagonal, duplicate or missing identifiers, data after \texttt{EOF}) and tolerant about the rest (spacing, blank lines, Windows line endings, unknown header entries, a missing \texttt{EOF}). It reads from any \texttt{std::istream}, which lets the tests feed it small strings.

\section{Algorithms}\label{sec-algorithms}

This section explains how each method works, with the code that carries its idea. The listings are taken from \texttt{src/}; long comments are shortened and a few lines are joined to save space. \subsection{Trivial method}\label{sec-trivial}

\texttt{TrivialSolver} returns the indices $0, 1, \dots, n-1$. The parsers store each city at the position given by its identifier, so this is the tour $1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow n$ required by the subject, whatever the order of the lines in the file.

\subsection{Held-Karp}\label{sec-hk}

Held-Karp~\cite{heldkarp} is exact. City 0 is the fixed start, and $c(S,k)$ is the length of the shortest path that leaves city 0, visits every city of the set $S$ exactly once and ends at city $k \in S$. Such a path is a shortest path to some city $i$ through $S \setminus \{k\}$, followed by the edge $(i,k)$, which gives the recurrence of the subject:
\[
c(\{k\},k) = d(0,k), \qquad c(S,k) = \min_{i \in S \setminus \{k\}} \big( c(S \setminus \{k\}, i) + d(i,k) \big),
\]
and the optimal length is $\min_k c(V \setminus \{0\}, k) + d(k,0)$. The table is one flat vector: its row is the bitmask of $S$ and its column the end city, so $c(S,k)$ is stored in \texttt{cost[S * m + k]}, with $m = n-1$. It is filled by increasing size of $S$; within one size, \texttt{SubsetIterator} lists the subsets with Gosper's hack (Table~\ref{tab-subset}), so every value a cell needs is already computed. To recover the tour (Figure~\ref{fig-hk}), the solver walks back from the best last city: at each step it looks for the city $i$ whose stored value plus $d(i,k)$ gives exactly the value of the current cell. The comparison with \texttt{==} is safe because it adds the same two numbers as when the table was filled. Two practical details: the distances are first copied into a plain matrix, since they are read about $n^2 2^n$ times, and the solver refuses more than 24 cities (Table~\ref{tab-extra}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}
\vspace{0pt}
{\footnotesize\raggedright\textbf{Filling the table} (\texttt{HeldKarpSolver.cpp})\par}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=side]
// city 0 is the start, element e of a subset is city e + 1
std::vector<double> cost((1ULL << m) * m, infinity);

for (std::size_t k = 0; k < m; k++) {
  subset::Subset single(0, m);
  single.add(k);
  cost[single.index() * m + k] = d[0][k + 1];
}

for (std::size_t size = 2; size <= m; size++) {
  for (subset::SubsetIterator it(m, size);
       it != subset::SubsetIterator::end(); ++it) {
    const subset::Subset& s = *it;

    for (std::size_t k : s) {
      subset::Subset rest = s;
      rest.minus(k);

      double best = infinity;
      for (std::size_t i : rest) {
        best = std::min(best, cost[rest.index() * m + i]
                              + d[i + 1][k + 1]);
      }
      cost[s.index() * m + k] = best;
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}
\vspace{0pt}
{\footnotesize\raggedright\textbf{Backtracking} (\texttt{HeldKarpSolver.cpp})\par}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=side]
// pick the last city before going back to city 0
const subset::Subset all(m, m);
std::size_t last = 0;
for (std::size_t k = 1; k < m; k++) {
  if (cost[all.index() * m + k] + d[k + 1][0]
      < cost[all.index() * m + last] + d[last + 1][0]) {
    last = k;
  }
}

// walk back: which city came before each one?
Tour tour;
subset::Subset s = all;
std::size_t k = last;
while (true) {
  tour.push_back(k + 1);
  subset::Subset rest = s;
  rest.minus(k);
  if (rest.index() == 0) { break; }
  for (std::size_t i : rest) {
    // same sum as when the table was filled: == is exact
    if (cost[rest.index() * m + i] + d[i + 1][k + 1]
        == cost[s.index() * m + k]) { k = i; break; }
  }
  s = rest;
}
tour.push_back(0);
std::reverse(tour.begin(), tour.end());
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Minimum spanning tree tour}\label{sec-mst}

\texttt{MstConstructor} builds a minimum spanning tree with Prim's algorithm and visits it in depth-first order (Figure~\ref{fig-mst}). Prim grows the tree from city 0. An array keeps, for every city outside the tree, its cheapest edge to the tree, so each step picks the closest city in $O(n)$ and then updates the array with the edges of the new city. On a complete graph this $O(n^2)$ version is the natural choice, since there are $n(n-1)/2$ edges anyway. The children of each city are sorted, so the result is deterministic. The tour is the order in which a depth-first walk first meets the cities. We use an explicit stack instead of recursion, so a tree shaped like a long path cannot overflow the call stack.

\begin{figure}[H]
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.52\linewidth}
\vspace{0pt}
{\footnotesize\raggedright\textbf{Prim's algorithm} (\texttt{MinimumSpanningTree.cpp})\par}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=side]
for (std::size_t added = 0; added < n; added++) {
  // the closest vertex outside the tree joins it
  std::size_t next = n;
  for (std::size_t v = 0; v < n; v++) {
    if (!inTree[v] && (next == n
                       || cheapest[v] < cheapest[next])) {
      next = v;
    }
  }
  inTree[next] = true;
  totalWeight += cheapest[next];
  if (next != root) {
    children[parent[next]].push_back(next);
  }
  // the new vertex may be a cheaper link for the others
  for (std::size_t v = 0; v < n; v++) {
    if (inTree[v]) { continue; }
    const double w = graph.weight(next, v);
    if (w < cheapest[v]) { cheapest[v] = w; parent[v] = next; }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.46\linewidth}
\vspace{0pt}
{\footnotesize\raggedright\textbf{Depth-first order} (\texttt{MstConstructor.cpp})\par}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=side]
const MinimumSpanningTree tree(graph);

// explicit stack instead of recursion; a tree has
// no cycle, so no "visited" list is needed
Tour tour;
tour.reserve(graph.size());
std::vector<std::size_t> toVisit{
    MinimumSpanningTree::root};

while (!toVisit.empty()) {
  const std::size_t vertex = toVisit.back();
  toVisit.pop_back();
  tour.push_back(vertex);

  // pushed in reverse: smallest child first
  const auto& children = tree.childrenOf(vertex);
  toVisit.insert(toVisit.end(), children.rbegin(),
                 children.rend());
}
return tour;
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\caption{Minimum spanning tree tour: Prim's algorithm, then a depth-first walk with an explicit stack.}
\label{fig-mst}
\end{figure}

A walk around the tree uses every edge twice, so its length is $2\,\mathrm{MST}$. The depth-first order skips the cities already visited, and with the triangle inequality each shortcut is no longer than the part of the walk it replaces. Hence the tour is at most $2\,\mathrm{MST} \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$, because removing one edge from an optimal tour leaves a spanning tree. The bound needs the triangle inequality, which none of the five matrix files satisfies: in \texttt{gr17}, for example, going directly from city 1 to city 6 costs 150, but going through city 7 costs only 143. Our tests therefore check the bound on the coordinate files only.

\subsection{Nearest neighbour and 2-opt}\label{sec-nn2opt}

\texttt{NearestNeighbourConstructor} starts at city 0 and always moves to the closest city not visited yet; on equal distances it keeps the smallest index, so the result is deterministic. It takes $O(n^2)$ time but, unlike the MST tour, it has no guarantee: its last edges often have to cross the whole map to reach the cities left behind.

\texttt{TwoOpt} then improves either tour (Figure~\ref{fig-nn2opt}). For two edges $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ of the tour, it computes the change of length if they are replaced by $(a,c)$ and $(b,d)$; when the change is negative, reversing the part of the tour from $b$ to $c$ applies the move. Each pass tries every pair of non-adjacent edges and applies an improving move as soon as it finds one, and passes repeat until a whole pass finds none. The result is a local optimum: no single exchange of two edges makes it shorter, although a larger change might (Section~\ref{sec-perf}). A change must be below $-10^{-9}$ to count, so that rounding noise on non-integer distances can never make two equally long tours swap forever.

\begin{figure}[H]
\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.45\linewidth}

// change of length when (t[i], t[i+1]) and (t[j], t[j+1])
// are replaced by (t[i], t[j]) and (t[i+1], t[j+1])
double gain(const tsp::Graph& graph, const tsp::Tour& tour,
            std::size_t i, std::size_t j) {
  const std::size_t a = tour[i];
  const std::size_t b = tour[i + 1];
  const std::size_t c = tour[j];
  const std::size_t d = tour[(j + 1) % tour.size()];
  return graph.weight(a, c) + graph.weight(b, d)
       - graph.weight(a, b) - graph.weight(c, d);
}

// in improve(graph, tour), epsilon = 1e-9:
for (std::size_t pass = 0; improved && pass < maxPasses;
     pass++) {
  improved = false;
  for (std::size_t i = 0; i + 2 < n; i++) {
    for (std::size_t j = i + 2; j < n; j++) {
      // (t[0], t[1]) and (t[n-1], t[0]) share t[0]
      if (i == 0 && j == n - 1) { continue; }
      if (gain(graph, tour, i, j) < -epsilon) {
        applyMove(tour, i, j);   // reverse t[i+1..j]
        improved = true;
      }
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Held-Karp}

With A fixed as the start, the subsets contain B, C, D and E, stored as bits 0 to 3: the set $\{B, D\}$ is \texttt{0101} $= 5$ and the full set is \texttt{1111} $= 15$. Table~\ref{tab-trace-hk} is the whole table. Its row is the subset $S$ and its column the end city $k$; each cell gives $c(S,k)$ and, in brackets, the city visited just before $k$. The rows of size 1 are the distances from A, and the rows of each size are filled in the order of Gosper's hack: 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12, then 7, 11, 13, 14, then 15. For example,
$c(\{C,D,E\}, C) = \min\big(c(\{D,E\}, D) + d(D,C),\; c(\{D,E\}, E) + d(E,C)\big) = \min(10 + 4,\; 17 + 6) = 14$, through D.
To close the tour, the solver adds the edge back to A (Table~\ref{tab-trace-close}). B and E both give 23, because they are the same cycle in opposite directions, and B is kept since a later city must be strictly shorter to replace it. Walking back from $(\texttt{1111}, B)$ then finds D, C and E (Table~\ref{tab-trace-back}), so the tour is A E C D B, of length 23.

\subsection{Nearest neighbour and minimum spanning tree tours, then 2-opt}

From A, nearest neighbour always moves to the closest unvisited city: E, then B, D and C, and finally back to A (Table~\ref{tab-trace-nn}). The last edge, C--A, costs 8 because nothing is left near C, and the tour A E B D C has length 26. For the MST tour, Prim grows the tree from A (Table~\ref{tab-trace-prim}): E joins first, then B, then C, whose cheapest link has dropped from 8 (through A) to 6 (through E), and finally D. The tree weighs 16. The depth-first walk visits A, then its children B and E in increasing order. B has no child, so the walk continues with E, C and D, which gives the tour A B E C D of length 28, within the bound $2 \times 16 = 32$ of Section~\ref{sec-mst}.

Both tours contain a crossing, made of the edges drawn dashed in Figure~\ref{fig-traced}. In the first pass, 2-opt scans the pairs of edges $(i, j)$ and finds that only the pair $(1, 4)$ shortens either tour (Table~\ref{tab-trace-2opt}). It removes E--B and C--A from the nearest neighbour tour (gain 3), and B--E and D--A from the MST tour (gain 5). Reversing the positions 2 to 4 gives A E C D B and A B D C E, both of length 23: the cycle found by Held-Karp, the second one traversed in the opposite direction. A second pass finds no improving move, so 2-opt stops. On such a small instance one move is enough; on larger ones it stops at a local optimum, as Section~\ref{sec-perf} shows.

\section{Complexity}\label{sec-complexity}

Table~\ref{tab-complexity} gives the time and memory cost of each part, for an instance of $n$ cities. For Held-Karp, the three nested loops run over the $2^{n-1}$ subsets, the $n-1$ possible end cities and the $n-2$ possible previous cities, which gives the $O(n^2 2^n)$ time of the subject; the table holds $(n-1)\,2^{n-1}$ values, hence $O(n 2^n)$ memory, and this memory is what limits the solver to 24 cities. The heuristics are polynomial: building a tour costs $O(n^2)$, like reading a matrix file, and so does each pass of 2-opt. How many passes 2-opt needs depends on the instance and is not bounded in advance; Section~\ref{sec-perf} shows the times we measured.

\section{Performance evaluation}\label{sec-perf}

\subsection{Method}

We measured the execution time, the solution quality and the scalability of the four methods with \texttt{benchmark.py}. Every number in this section comes from the two files it writes, \texttt{output/results\_provided.csv} and \texttt{output/results\_scaling.csv}, and \texttt{plot\_results.py} draws the figures from them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Time} is the wall-clock time of a whole run (start, reading, solving, writing), averaged over 5 runs after one warm-up run, on an Apple M5 laptop with 24\,GB of memory (macOS, Apple clang~21, \texttt{-O3}). About 1.5\,ms of every run is a fixed cost, which is what the trivial method shows, so differences below a millisecond are not meaningful.
  \item \textbf{Quality} is the gap $(\mathrm{length} - \mathrm{optimum}) / \mathrm{optimum}$. The script recomputes each length from the tour file with the TSPLIB formulas. The optima are the values published with TSPLIB~\cite{tsplib}; for our 5-city example, the optimum (23) comes from trying every tour.
  \item \textbf{Scalability} is measured on generated instances, one per size, with cities placed uniformly at random in a $1000 \times 1000$ square: $n = 8, 10, \dots, 22$, the range of Held-Karp, and $n = 200, 500, 2000$ for the heuristics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scalability}

\subsection{Summary}

The measurements show the trade-off we expected. Held-Karp is exact but limited to 24 cities by its memory, and takes 2.8\,s at that size. The two heuristics handle every instance we tried, up to 2\,000 cities, in at most 135\,ms, and stay on average 5.6\,\% and 5.7\,\% above the optimum on the provided instances. The trivial method only measures the fixed cost of a run.

\section{Reflections}

Three lessons stand out. First, the redesign we did before the mid-term paid off: every method of the final version was added as new classes, without touching the parser or the graph classes, and splitting a heuristic into a construction and an improvement turned one idea into four methods. This time every algorithm also came with its tests in the same commit, a lesson from the mid-term.

Second, the hardest decisions were not about code. Choosing the third method took a full day of reading before we wrote a line, and the memory of Held-Karp, more than its time, turned out to be its real limit. The measurements made the trade-off concrete: an exact answer for 24 cities costs 2.8 seconds, while 2-opt gets within 10\,\% of the optimum on 1\,002 cities in 40 milliseconds.

Third, we would integrate more often. Held-Karp and the heuristics were developed on separate branches and only came together on 9 and 14 November, which left little time to test them side by side. Merging every week would have shown integration problems earlier.
